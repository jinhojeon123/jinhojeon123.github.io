## Intro

This post covers Chapter 2 of Folland’s *Real Analysis*: **Integration**.  
We define the Lebesgue integral for nonnegative and complex‑valued functions, prove the central convergence theorems, and explore modes of convergence. The chapter also introduces product measures, the $n$-dimensional Lebesgue integral, and integration in polar coordinates.

---

## 2.1 Measurable Functions (Review)

### Simple functions
A **simple function** on a measure space $(X,\mathcal{M},\mu)$ is a finite linear combination of characteristic functions of measurable sets:
$$
\phi(x) = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \chi_{E_k}(x), \qquad E_k \in \mathcal{M},\; a_k \ge 0.
$$
Every nonnegative measurable function is the pointwise limit of an increasing sequence of simple functions.

---

## 2.2 Integration of Nonnegative Functions

### Integral of a simple function
For a simple function $\phi = \sum a_k \chi_{E_k}$ with disjoint $E_k$,
$$
\int \phi \, d\mu = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \, \mu(E_k),
$$
using the convention $0 \cdot \infty = 0$.

### Integral of a nonnegative measurable function
For any nonnegative measurable function $f \ge 0$,
$$
\int f \, d\mu := \sup\left\{ \int \phi \, d\mu : 0 \le \phi \le f,\; \phi \text{ simple} \right\}.
$$
The integral takes values in $[0,\infty]$.

**Basic properties**  
- **Linearity**: $\int (af + bg) = a\int f + b\int g$ for $a,b \ge 0$.  
- **Monotonicity**: $f \le g \implies \int f \le \int g$.  
- **Vanishing**: $\int f = 0$ if and only if $f = 0$ almost everywhere.

### Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT)
If $\{f_n\}$ is an increasing sequence of nonnegative measurable functions ($f_1 \le f_2 \le \cdots$) converging pointwise to $f$, then
$$
\int f = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int f_n.
$$

### Fatou’s Lemma
For any sequence $\{f_n\}$ of nonnegative measurable functions,
$$
\int \liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n \le \liminf_{n\to\infty} \int f_n.
$$

## 2.3 Integration of Complex Functions

Now we extend to complex‑valued functions. If $f = u + iv$ with $u, v$ real‑valued, we define
$$
\int f \, d\mu = \int u^+ d\mu - \int u^- d\mu + i\left( \int v^+ d\mu - \int v^- d\mu \right),
$$
provided $\int |f| \, d\mu < \infty$. Such an $f$ is called **integrable**, and the collection of all integrable complex functions is denoted $L^1(\mu)$.

**Properties**  
- $\left| \int f \, d\mu \right| \le \int |f| \, d\mu$.  
- $L^1(\mu)$ is a vector space, and the integral is a linear functional.

### Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT)
Let $\{f_n\}$ be a sequence of measurable functions converging pointwise (a.e.) to $f$. If there exists an integrable function $g$ such that $|f_n| \le g$ a.e. for all $n$, then
$$
\int f = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int f_n,
$$
and moreover $\int |f_n - f| \to 0$ (convergence in $L^1$).

---

## 2.4 Modes of Convergence

The convergence theorems are better appreciated with an understanding of different convergence types:

- **Pointwise almost everywhere (a.e.)**
- **Almost uniform convergence**: For any $\epsilon > 0$ there exists a set $E_\epsilon$ with $\mu(E_\epsilon) < \epsilon$ such that $f_n \to f$ uniformly on $E_\epsilon^c$.
- **Convergence in measure**: $\mu(\{x : |f_n(x)-f(x)| \ge \epsilon\}) \to 0$ for every $\epsilon>0$.
- **Convergence in $L^1$**: $\int |f_n - f| \to 0$.

**Egorov’s Theorem**  
On a finite measure space, pointwise a.e. convergence implies almost uniform convergence.

**Relationships**  
- Almost uniform convergence $\Rightarrow$ a.e. convergence and convergence in measure.  
- A sequence converging in measure has a subsequence that converges almost uniformly.  
- $L^1$ convergence $\Rightarrow$ convergence in measure (Chebyshev’s inequality).  
- The DCT turns pointwise a.e. convergence + domination into $L^1$ convergence.

## 2.5 Product Measures

Given two $\sigma$-finite measure spaces $(X,\mathcal{M},\mu)$ and $(Y,\mathcal{N},\nu)$, the **product $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N}$ is generated by the measurable rectangles $A \times B$ ($A \in \mathcal{M}, B \in \mathcal{N}$).  
There exists a unique **product measure** $\mu \times \nu$ satisfying
$$
(\mu \times \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A)\,\nu(B)
$$
for all such rectangles. It is constructed via the Carathéodory extension theorem.

---

## 2.6 The $n$-Dimensional Lebesgue Integral

The Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$, denoted $m^n$, is the completion of the $n$-fold product of the one‑dimensional Lebesgue measure. This identification allows us to use the **Fubini–Tonelli Theorem**:

- **Tonelli’s Theorem**: If $f \ge 0$ is measurable (with respect to the product $\sigma$-algebra), then
  $$
  \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f \, d m^n = \int \! \cdots \! \int f(x_1,\dots,x_n) \, dx_1 \cdots dx_n,
  $$
  and the iterated integrals are measurable functions of the remaining variables (defined almost everywhere).
- **Fubini’s Theorem**: If $f \in L^1(m^n)$, the same equality holds, and for almost every fixed value of $(x_2,\dots,x_n)$, the function $x_1 \mapsto f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is in $L^1$ and the iterated integral is in $L^1$ as well.

## 2.7 Integration in Polar Coordinates

On $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$ we can write $x = r\omega$ with $r > 0$ and $\omega \in S^{n-1}$. Lebesgue measure factors as
$$
dx = r^{n-1} \, dr \, d\sigma(\omega),
$$
where $\sigma$ is the surface measure on the unit sphere $S^{n-1}$. Hence for any nonnegative (or integrable) function $f$,
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) \, dx = \int_0^\infty \int_{S^{n-1}} f(r\omega) \, r^{n-1} \, d\sigma(\omega) \, dr.
$$
A typical application is the computation of the volume $\omega_n$ of the unit ball and the surface area $n\omega_n$ of $S^{n-1}$, leading to $\omega_n = \frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2 + 1)}$.

## Summary – Chapter 2

1. The Lebesgue integral was defined for nonnegative functions via simple‑function approximation.  
2. **MCT** and **Fatou’s Lemma** provide the first limit‑interchange tools.  
3. Integration was extended to complex functions, giving the space $L^1$ and the **DCT**.  
4. The main modes of convergence were compared, with **Egorov’s Theorem** bridging pointwise and uniform approximations.  
5. **Product measures** were constructed, paving the way for **Fubini–Tonelli**.  
6. The $n$-dimensional Lebesgue integral reduces to iterated $1$-dimensional integrals.  
7. **Polar coordinates** simplify integrals with radial symmetry.
