# Fatou's Lemma

## Statement

Let $\{f_n\}$ be a sequence of nonnegative measurable functions, i.e. $f_n \in L^+$.

Then

$$
\int \liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty} \int f_n.
$$

## Proof

Define

$$
g_n(x)
=
\inf_{k\ge n} f_k(x).
$$

Then

$$
0 \le g_n \le g_{n+1}.
$$

Hence $\{g_n\}$ is an increasing sequence of nonnegative measurable functions.

Moreover,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} g_n
=
\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n.
$$

Therefore, by the Monotone Convergence Theorem,

$$
\int \liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n
=
\int \lim_{n\to\infty} g_n
=
\lim_{n\to\infty} \int g_n.
$$

For every $k\ge n$,

$$
g_n \le f_k.
$$

By monotonicity of the integral,

$$
\int g_n
\le
\int f_k
\qquad
\forall k\ge n.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\int g_n
\le
\inf_{k\ge n} \int f_k.
$$

Taking the limit as $n\to\infty$,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int g_n
\le
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\inf_{k\ge n}\int f_k.
$$

By the definition of $\liminf$,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\inf_{k\ge n} a_k
=
\liminf_{n\to\infty} a_n.
$$

Hence,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int g_n
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int f_n.
$$

Since

$$
\int \liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n
=
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int g_n,
$$

we conclude that

$$
\boxed{
\int \liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
}.
\qquad \square
$$

## Intuition

Fatou's Lemma says that the integral cannot suddenly become larger when we pass to a pointwise lower limit.

The key construction is

$$
g_n = \inf_{k\ge n} f_k.
$$

Each $g_n$ represents the lowest value among the tail

$$
f_n, f_{n+1}, f_{n+2}, \ldots
$$

and these tail infima increase:

$$
g_1 \le g_2 \le g_3 \le \cdots.
$$

Their limit is exactly

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty} f_n.
$$

Thus Fatou's Lemma is essentially an application of the Monotone Convergence Theorem to the sequence of tail infima.

## Remarks

Fatou's Lemma does not require $f_n$ to converge.

It only requires

$$
f_n \ge 0.
$$

The inequality may be strict.

In general,

$$
\int \liminf f_n
<
\liminf \int f_n
$$

is possible.

# Dominated Convergence Theorem

## Statement

Let $\{f_n\}$ be a sequence of measurable functions such that

$$
f_n \to f
\qquad \text{a.e.}
$$

Suppose there exists $g\in L^1$ such that

$$
|f_n| \le g
\qquad \text{a.e. for every }n.
$$

Then

$$
f\in L^1
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
=
\int f
}.
$$

## Proof

Since

$$
f_n \to f
\qquad \text{a.e.},
$$

we also have

$$
|f_n|
\to
|f|
\qquad \text{a.e.}
$$

Since

$$
|f_n|\le g,
$$

taking the pointwise limit gives

$$
|f|\le g
\qquad \text{a.e.}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\int |f|
\le
\int g
<
\infty.
$$

Hence

$$
f\in L^1.
$$

Now, from

$$
|f_n|\le g,
$$

we obtain

$$
-g\le f_n\le g.
$$

Thus

$$
g+f_n\ge0
$$

and

$$
g-f_n\ge0.
$$

We can therefore apply Fatou's Lemma to both sequences.

### Step 1: Apply Fatou to $g+f_n$

Since

$$
g+f_n \to g+f
\qquad \text{a.e.},
$$

Fatou's Lemma gives

$$
\int(g+f)
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\int(g+f_n).
$$

Since $g\in L^1$,

$$
\int g+\int f
\le
\int g
+
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int f_n.
$$

Subtracting $\int g$,

$$
\boxed{
\int f
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
}.
\tag{1}
$$

### Step 2: Apply Fatou to $g-f_n$

Similarly,

$$
g-f_n \to g-f
\qquad \text{a.e.}
$$

and Fatou's Lemma gives

$$
\int(g-f)
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\int(g-f_n).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\int g-\int f
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
\left(
\int g-\int f_n
\right).
$$

Using

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}(C-a_n)
=
C-\limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n,
$$

we obtain

$$
\int g-\int f
\le
\int g
-
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int f_n.
$$

Subtracting $\int g$ and multiplying by $-1$,

$$
\boxed{
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
\le
\int f
}.
\tag{2}
$$

Combining (1) and (2),

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
\le
\int f
\le
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int f_n.
$$

But for every sequence,

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty} a_n
\le
\limsup_{n\to\infty} a_n.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
=
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
=
\int f.
$$

Hence,

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
=
\int f
}.
\qquad \square
$$

## Why Domination Is Necessary

Pointwise convergence alone does not allow us to interchange the limit and the integral.

Consider

$$
f_n(x)
=
n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}(x)
$$

on $(0,1)$.

For every $x>0$,

$$
f_n(x)\to0.
$$

Thus

$$
f_n\to0
\qquad \text{a.e.}
$$

However,

$$
\int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx
=
1
$$

for every $n$.

Therefore,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^1 f_n(x)\,dx
=
1,
$$

while

$$
\int_0^1 \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)\,dx
=
0.
$$

Hence,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
\neq
\int\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n.
$$

The domination condition prevents this type of concentration of mass.

## Comparison with MCT and Fatou's Lemma

### Monotone Convergence Theorem

Assumptions:

$$
0\le f_n\uparrow f.
$$

Conclusion:

$$
\int f_n
\to
\int f.
$$

The sequence must be nonnegative and monotone.

### Fatou's Lemma

Assumption:

$$
f_n\ge0.
$$

Conclusion:

$$
\int\liminf f_n
\le
\liminf\int f_n.
$$

No convergence or monotonicity is required.

### Dominated Convergence Theorem

Assumptions:

$$
f_n\to f
\quad\text{a.e.}
$$

and

$$
|f_n|\le g
\qquad
\text{for some }g\in L^1.
$$

Conclusion:

$$
\int f_n
\to
\int f.
$$

Thus DCT trades monotonicity for the existence of an integrable dominating function.

## Examples

Consider

$$
f_n(x)
=
\frac{x}{n}
$$

on $[0,1]$.

For every $x\in\[0,1\]$,

$$
f_n(x)\to0.
$$

Also,

$$
|f_n(x)|
=
\frac{x}{n}
\le
x.
$$

Since

$$
g(x)=x\in L^1([0,1]),
$$

DCT gives

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}
\int_0^1\frac{x}{n}\,dx
=
\int_0^1 0\,dx
=
0.
$$

Indeed,

$$
\int_0^1\frac{x}{n}\,dx
=
\frac{1}{2n}
\to0.
$$

# Summary

The three main results are closely related.

$$
\boxed{
\text{MCT}
\Longrightarrow
\text{Fatou's Lemma}
\Longrightarrow
\text{DCT}
}
$$

The Monotone Convergence Theorem handles increasing nonnegative sequences.

Fatou's Lemma extends this idea to arbitrary nonnegative sequences by introducing the tail infimum

$$
g_n
=
\inf_{k\ge n}f_k.
$$

The Dominated Convergence Theorem then uses Fatou's Lemma twice, applied to

$$
g+f_n
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
g-f_n,
$$

to obtain both

$$
\int f
\le
\liminf\int f_n
$$

and

$$
\limsup\int f_n
\le
\int f.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n
=
\int f
}.
$$
